### A1 — Brown 2008 (Ann. Appl. Stat.)

**Citation.** Brown, Lawrence D. (2008). "In-season prediction of batting averages: A field test of empirical Bayes and Bayes methodologies." The Annals of Applied Statistics, Vol. 2, No. 1, pp. 113-152. DOI: 10.1214/07-AOAS138. arXiv:0803.3697 [stat.AP].

**Access used.** Full text — fetched the open arXiv PDF (arXiv:0803.3697, 42 pages), extracted all pages locally with pypdf, and read the complete paper including Tables 1-7 and the appendix.

**Objective.** Use each MLB player's batting record from an early portion of the 2005 season (primarily the first three months, April-June) to estimate his latent hitting ability p_i and predict his batting average for the remainder of the season, then validate against the actual second-half outcomes. The study has a dual focus: practical accuracy of in-season BA prediction, and a field test comparing empirical Bayes, hierarchical Bayes, James-Stein, group-mean, and naive estimators on real data. Secondary questions include whether pitchers and nonpitchers should be modeled separately and whether the constant-p binomial model (no streakiness) holds within a season.

**Method.** Monthly at-bat/hit totals for every 2005 MLB player; hits modeled as Bin(N_i, p_i). Each record is transformed with a mean-matching variance-stabilizing transform X = arcsin sqrt((H+1/4)/(N+1/2)), so X ~ N(theta_i, 1/(4N_i)) approximately — heteroscedastic with KNOWN variance (the c=1/4 constant is chosen to nearly eliminate bias for N as small as 10, unlike the classical c=0 or Anscombe c=3/8 forms). Players need >10 first-half at-bats for estimation (P=567 all batters; 486 nonpitchers, 81 pitchers) and >10 second-half at-bats for validation (P=499; 435/64). Estimators compared: naive (first-half value predicts second half), group mean, parametric EB by method of moments EB(MM), parametric EB by maximum likelihood EB(ML) (an Efron-Morris variant), a new nonparametric EB (NPEB, Brown-Greenshtein kernel estimator of the marginal density plugged into Brown's 1971 posterior-mean formula), a formal hierarchical Bayes estimator under the harmonic prior (HB), and a heteroscedastic positive-part James-Stein. Performance metric: TSE*, the estimated total squared prediction error normalized so the naive estimator = 1 (an unbiased de-noising of sum of squared prediction error), plus an at-bat-weighted variant TWSE* and a batting-average-scale variant TSE*_R. Section 7 tests the constant-p independent-binomial assumption via z-scores between halves and chi-square statistics across months, with FDR control; an appendix repeats the test on 10-day segments.

**Results.** Table 2 (all batters, first half -> second half, TSE*; naive=1): group mean 0.852, EB(MM) 0.593, EB(ML) 0.902, NPEB 0.508, harmonic prior 0.884, James-Stein 0.525. The naive predictor is the WORST method — simply predicting every batter at the overall mean (first-half mean BA 0.240) beats using each player's own first-half average. The best method (NPEB) cuts squared error about 40% relative to the overall mean. Within homogeneous subgroups (Table 3, TSE*): nonpitchers — mean 0.378, EB(MM) 0.387, EB(ML) 0.398, NPEB 0.372, HB 0.391, J-S 0.359; pitchers — mean 0.127, EB(MM) 0.129, EB(ML) 0.117, NPEB 0.212, HB 0.128, J-S 0.164; i.e., within subgroups the group mean is nearly unbeatable and the EB estimators shrink almost fully to it. One month of data (Table 4, all batters): mean 0.250, EB(MM) 0.240, NPEB 0.169, J-S 0.218 vs naive 1 — one-month averages are almost pure noise. Five months predicting the last month (nonpitchers, Table 5): naive 0.955... actually naive=1, mean 0.955, EB(MM) 0.904, NPEB 0.944, J-S 0.808 — with 5 months of data the naive average finally nearly matches the mean. Break-even calculation: ~3.6 months of data are needed before a player's own average matches the overall mean for all batters; ~1.5 seasons for nonpitchers and ~3.4 seasons for pitchers before an individual's average beats his group mean. Shrinkage weights are N-dependent (sigma_i^2 = 1/(4N_i)): low-AB players get shrunk much harder. EB(ML)/HB underperform on the pooled sample because {N_1i} and {X_1i} are correlated (R^2 = 0.247 overall; 0.18 among nonpitchers) and the X distribution is a two-component mixture (pitchers ~0.153 BA vs nonpitchers ~0.255); NPEB wins on the pooled sample precisely because it adapts to the nonnormal mixture. Model validation: monthly hit totals are consistent with independent binomials with constant p per batter (half-season KS p=0.046, no FDR discoveries at q*=0.05 for half-season or monthly splits), but at 10-day granularity 32 of 419 batters are flagged as genuinely "streaky" at q*=0.05.

**Conclusions.** Early-season individual batting averages are extremely noisy: used raw they are worse than ignoring the player entirely and predicting the group mean, and effective prediction requires N-dependent shrinkage toward an appropriate group mean (or a nonparametric EB fit when the population is a mixture). Within homogeneous player groups, simple shrinkage nearly to the group mean is close to optimal; the constant-ability binomial model holds well at month-plus granularity. Transferability to this MLB project is direct — same league, same statistic family — the main translation being that the project's early-season team/batter RATE features (April-June, exactly Brown's first-half window) should be treated as heavily-shrunk EB estimates rather than raw rates, with shrinkage weight tied to the pregame at-bat/PA count.

**Proposed experiments:**
- **EB(MM) shrinkage of early-season rate features with N-dependent weights** (→ E9a/b; metric: Win accuracy / AUC on March-June slice (vs current .561/.581 overall); batter model per-PA log-loss)
  For each in-season team/batter rate feature (team runs-scored rate, batter per-PA outcome rates), add a flag-gated shrunken variant: apply the arcsin sqrt((H+1/4)/(N+1/2)) transform, estimate (mu, tau^2) by Brown's method-of-moments (eq 4.6) from the current point-in-time league cross-section, and shrink with weight tau^2/(tau^2 + 1/(4N)). This replaces any fixed-constant shrinkage in the batter model's EB rate features with a sample-size-adaptive one. Evaluate walk-forward with the March-June split highlighted, since that is where the model trails the market.
- **Brown-calibrated early-season blend weight for prior-season rates** (→ E9a/b; metric: Early-season (Mar-Jun) win accuracy and totals MAE vs market; significance test vs current E9 candidate)
  E9a/b currently needs a decay/blend schedule for prior-season rates. Use Brown's break-even arithmetic as the prior: a hitter's own current-season rate only matches the group mean after ~1.5 seasons of PAs (~3.6 months for a heterogeneous pool), so the blend weight on current-season rate should be w = tau^2/(tau^2 + 1/(4N_PA)) with the complement on the prior-season (decayed) rate rather than a calendar-date ramp. Grid the tau^2 estimate (per-season MM fit vs pooled multi-season) as the only tunable. Point-in-time safe since N_PA and prior-season rates are pregame-known.
- **First-half/second-half TSE* harness for batter-model shrinkage selection** (→ B-series batter-model feature hygiene (NEW as a named experiment); metric: TSE* vs naive on held-out half-season; downstream per-PA log-loss in walkforward_batter)
  Reproduce Brown's validation design on project data as a standing diagnostic: for a past season, predict each batter's second-half (per-PA multinomial component) rates from first-half data under competing shrinkage schemes (raw, league mean, EB(MM), within-subgroup EB), scoring normalized TSE*. Run within homogeneous subgroups (lineup-slot or role buckets) since Brown shows pooled mixtures distort parametric EB — the modern analogue of his pitcher/nonpitcher split. Winner feeds the batter model's rate-feature builder.
- **Null-check on within-season recency decay for batter rates** (→ E13; metric: Walk-forward log-loss/accuracy delta with multi-seed confirmation; ship only decay^seasons_ago if within-season decay fails significance)
  Brown's Section 7 finds batter latent ability constant at month granularity in-season (no monthly streakiness survives FDR), implying within-season recency weighting of batter rate features should add nothing beyond noise; his 10-day-scale streakiness (32/419 batters) is too short-horizon to exploit as a pregame feature. Before investing in E13 variants that decay within a season, A/B a within-season decay against flat within-season weighting (decay only across seasons), expecting the flat variant to win or tie.

**Data needs.** Per-player at-bat/hit (and per-PA outcome) counts at monthly or finer granularity, point-in-time as of each game date — the project already owns this via the game/Statcast ingestion tables and the existing EB-shrunken rate feature pipeline. Team-level run/rate aggregates for E9a/b are already built by features.team_features. Nothing external must be acquired; the only new work is computing arcsin-transformed cross-sectional (mu, tau^2) method-of-moments fits point-in-time inside features.py-gated columns, and (for the TSE* harness) a one-off half-season split script over past seasons already in the DB.

**Caveats.** Single season of data (2005) and 18 years old; the author himself notes the estimator rankings beyond "naive is worst" may not be stable across seasons (simulation SDs of 0.05-0.20 on TSE* differences). Studies individual batting average only, not team run rates or the project's 8-class PA outcome — team-level rates accumulate N far faster, so shrinkage weights will be much closer to 1 for team features than the paper's batter-level weights suggest. The pitcher/nonpitcher mixture finding (which drives NPEB's headline win) is largely moot post-universal-DH; the within-homogeneous-group results (group mean + mild EB shrinkage nearly optimal) are the transferable part. The equal-weight-per-player criterion differs from a PA-weighted objective, and the paper shows the ranking shifts under weighting (J-S best at 0.502 TWSE*). The paper deliberately excludes prior-season data, so its blend guidance for E9a/b is an inference from its break-even calculations, not a tested prior-season blend. PDF text extraction had minor ligature artifacts but all quoted numbers were legible in the tables.

**Approval status:** APPROVED (user, 2026-07-22)

### A2 — Dixon & Coles 1997 (JRSS-C)

**Citation.** Dixon, M. J., & Coles, S. G. (1997). Modelling Association Football Scores and Inefficiencies in the Football Betting Market. Journal of the Royal Statistical Society: Series C (Applied Statistics), 46(2), 265-280.

**Access used.** Abstract via OUP article page (paywalled full text) + named secondary sources reproducing the model: dashee87.github.io tutorial (tau formulas, rho ~ -0.1285, phi(t)=exp(-xi t), paper's xi=0.0065 half-weekly vs replication 0.00325/day), opisthokonta.net R implementation (attack/defence/home parameterization, rho ~ -0.13, identifiability constraint), and web-search corroboration of the pseudo-likelihood estimation and the bet-when-probability-ratio-exceeds-threshold strategy (Semantic Scholar / Wiley abstract records).

**Objective.** Develop a parametric statistical model for association football match scores that is good enough to exploit potential inefficiencies in the UK football betting market. The model is fitted to English league and cup data from 1992-1995 and its predictive value is tested against bookmakers' odds from 1995-1996. The explicit test of success is whether a betting strategy built on the model's probabilities yields a positive return.

**Method.** Each match's score is modeled with Poisson rates built from per-team attack and defence parameters plus a home-advantage effect: home goals ~ Poisson(lambda) with lambda a function of the home team's attack, the away team's defence, and home advantage; away goals ~ Poisson(mu) from the away attack and home defence (an identifiability constraint is placed on the attack parameters). Two departures from a plain independent-Poisson model are the paper's signature contributions. First, a dependence correction tau(x, y), controlled by a parameter rho, adjusts the joint probability of only the four low-score outcomes 0-0, 1-0, 0-1, 1-1 (e.g. P(0-0) is scaled by 1 - lambda*mu*rho, P(1-1) by 1 - rho), because these scorelines are systematically mis-fit under independence; all other scorelines keep the product-Poisson probability. Second, to handle the dynamic nature of team ability, parameters are estimated by maximizing an exponentially time-weighted pseudo-likelihood at each prediction time point, with past matches down-weighted by phi(t) = exp(-xi*t); xi is chosen to maximize out-of-sample predictive quality rather than in-sample fit (secondary sources report the paper's chosen xi = 0.0065 with t in half-week units). Match outcome probabilities (home/draw/away) are then obtained by summing the corrected joint score distribution, and a betting strategy places a bet on an outcome when the ratio of the model probability to the bookmaker's implied probability exceeds a predetermined threshold.

**Results.** The abstract states that maximum likelihood estimates are computationally obtainable and that "the model is shown to have a positive return when used as the basis of a betting strategy" against 1995-1996 bookmakers' odds; secondary sources confirm the strategy was to bet only when the model-to-bookmaker probability ratio exceeded a threshold, with positive abnormal profit concentrated on outcomes the market overpriced. The specific threshold value and the return percentage in the original paper could not be verified from accessible sources and are omitted here. The rho estimate in the original paper is likewise not verifiable from the abstract; independent re-implementations on later English Premier League data consistently estimate rho at approximately -0.13 (negative, i.e. draws/low scores are under-predicted by an independent-Poisson model), and one open replication tuning the decay by out-of-sample predictive log-likelihood found an optimal xi of roughly 0.00325 per day on 5 seasons of EPL data, in the same regime as the paper's 0.0065 per half-week. These re-implementation numbers are corroborating context, not results of the 1997 paper itself.

**Conclusions.** The authors conclude that a relatively simple attack/defence Poisson model becomes practically useful once two realities are respected — scores are not quite independent (a targeted local correction on low scores suffices) and team ability drifts (recent matches must be up-weighted, with the decay rate chosen by predictive, not in-sample, criteria) — and that such a model was sharp enough to reveal exploitable inefficiency in the mid-1990s UK betting market. Transferability to MLB is credible but not literal: the two-Poisson-heads structure of this project is the direct descendant of this model, and both the "dependence concentrated in specific score regions" idea and the predictive-tuned exponential recency weighting carry over, though baseball's dependence structure differs mechanically (e.g. the home team not batting in the bottom of the 9th when leading compresses home winning margins) and the 1997 betting-market finding says nothing about today's far more efficient MLB market.

**Proposed experiments:**
- **Tau-style local dependence correction on the joint runs PMF** (→ E12; metric: p_home log-loss/Brier and AUC vs the .581 AUC / Phi(margin/sigma) baseline; walk-forward with significance test and multi-seed confirmation)
  Build the joint (home_runs, away_runs) PMF as the product of the two Poisson LightGBM heads, then apply a Dixon-Coles-style multiplicative correction tau(x, y; rho) on a small set of cells where independence demonstrably fails — for MLB the natural candidates are the low-margin cells (|margin| = 1, especially home-wins-by-1, given the bottom-of-the-9th truncation) rather than soccer's 0/1 cells. Estimate rho (or a 2-3 parameter cell-group version) by maximizing walk-forward out-of-sample log-likelihood of observed final scores, renormalize the PMF, and compute p_home by summing P(home > away) instead of Phi(margin/sigma). Diagnose first: tabulate observed-vs-product-PMF cell ratios on historical walk-forward predictions to see where the mass mismatch lives before parameterizing tau.
- **Predictively-tuned exponential recency weighting** (→ E13; metric: Walk-forward win accuracy / totals MAE vs unweighted baseline; xi = 0 must be rejected by significance test before shipping)
  Adopt the Dixon-Coles phi(t) = exp(-xi * t) sample-weighting in LightGBM training (sample_weight per game), with t in days rather than a coarse decay^seasons_ago, so the same xi governs within-season and across-season down-weighting continuously. Critically, follow their selection procedure: choose xi by maximizing walk-forward predictive log-likelihood on held-out future games, not training fit; sweep a grid bracketing the soccer-replication regime (roughly 0.001-0.01 per day) plus xi = 0 as the null. Flag-gate the weight column.
- **Continuous cross-season decay for early-season team rates** (→ E9a/b; metric: Win accuracy / AUC on March-June walk-forward slices vs current early-season blend; overall accuracy must not regress)
  Instead of a discrete prior-season blend coefficient, treat the offseason as elapsed calendar time under the same exp(-xi * t) weighting (optionally with an extra offseason discontinuity multiplier for roster turnover, estimated as one parameter). Prior-season games then enter early-season team-rate features with a principled, tuned weight that decays smoothly through April-June — directly targeting the March-June window where the model currently loses to the market.
- **Threshold-ratio ROI curve in the odds benchmark** (→ NEW (evaluation-only extension of benchmark_odds.py); metric: ROI-vs-threshold curves with bootstrap CIs; an edge is claimed only where the CI excludes the vig-implied negative baseline)
  Replicate the paper's evaluation design (not a model change): in scripts/benchmark_odds.py, compute for each game the ratio of model p_home (and totals-implied probabilities) to the closing-line implied probability, and report hypothetical flat-stake ROI as a function of the ratio threshold r, sliced by month. Odds remain evaluation-only, fully consistent with the hard rule. This gives a sharper, decision-relevant view of the March-June weakness and Aug-Oct edge than accuracy alone.

**Data needs.** Everything the four experiments need is already owned: historical final scores and game dates (games table), walk-forward prediction archives with the two Poisson head means (validation registry/preds), and closing lines in odds_lines for the benchmark-only ROI curve. The tau correction needs only the per-game (lambda_home, lambda_away) pairs plus observed scores; the recency and cross-season decay experiments need only game dates for the weight column. No new external data must be acquired; the paper itself required bookmaker odds, which the project already captures under the benchmark-only discipline.

**Caveats.** Full text unreachable: the exact betting threshold r, the return percentage, the paper's own rho and standard errors, and dataset details beyond the abstract could not be verified and are deliberately omitted; rho ~ -0.13 and xi values quoted are from later re-implementations on EPL data, not the 1997 fit. The source is 29 years old and studies soccer: goals are far rarer than runs, so the specific 0-0/1-0/0-1/1-1 correction cells do not transfer — only the method (local multiplicative correction where the product-Poisson PMF mis-fits) does, and the MLB mis-fit cells must be diagnosed empirically first (bottom-of-9th truncation makes home-win-by-1 the prime suspect). The positive-return finding reflects a mid-1990s UK fixed-odds market; it is evidence for the evaluation methodology, not for expected profitability against modern MLB closing lines. Their per-time-point pseudo-likelihood refit is computationally trivial for their model but corresponds to per-date retraining for LightGBM; the proposed sample-weight approximation is a pragmatic adaptation, not what the paper literally did.

**Approval status:** DENIED (user, 2026-07-22) — replaced by Karlis & Ntzoufras 2003 (round 2)

### A3 — Bukiet, Harold & Palacios 1997 (Oper. Res.)

**Citation.** Bukiet, B., Harold, E. R., & Palacios, J. L. (1997). "A Markov Chain Approach to Baseball." Operations Research, 45(1), 14-23. (Read via the open author preprint at https://web.njit.edu/~bukiet/Papers/ball.pdf)

**Access used.** Full text — the complete 22-page author PDF from web.njit.edu was downloaded, rendered page-by-page to images (the PDF's text layer has a broken character map), and read in full, including all equations and Tables 1-5.

**Objective.** Introduce a Markov chain method for evaluating baseball team offense built from individual, non-identical players, removing the identical-player restriction of earlier analytic models and the need for Monte Carlo simulation (Freeze 1974). The method is used to compute run distributions per half-inning and per nine-inning game for any specified lineup, to find optimal and near-optimal batting orders, to estimate expected season wins (the 1989 NL "pennant race"), and to quantify the effect of a specific trade on expected wins. The authors emphasize the framework is limited only by available data: any deterministic runner-advancement model can fill the transition matrix when detailed data is absent.

**Method.** A half-inning is in one of 25 states: 24 = 8 base-occupancy configurations (2^3) x 3 out counts, plus a 25th absorbing three-out state. Each batter gets a 25x25 transition matrix P with block upper-triangular structure: 8x8 A-blocks (events not increasing outs), B-blocks (outs +1), C-blocks (0 to 2 outs, e.g., double plays), and 8x1 vectors D0/E1/F2 into the absorbing state; transitions that decrease outs have probability zero. Under the D'Esopo-Lefkowitz (1960) deterministic advancement model, entries are the batter's event probabilities P_W, P_S, P_D, P_T, P_H, P_out (walk/single/double/triple/HR/out) with rules: out — runners do not advance; walk — advance only if forced; single — runner on 1st to 2nd, other runners score; double — runner on 1st to 3rd, others score; triple — all runners score; HR — everyone scores. Runs per transition are tracked by decomposing each P into P0..P4 (transitions scoring 0-4 runs) and propagating a 21-row (0-20 runs) x 25-column state-run matrix U_n; a nine-inning game uses 189 rows and iterates U0 P1 P2 ... P9 P1 ... until P(27 outs) > 0.999, capping team runs at 20. The 25th column of the final matrix is the run distribution for that lineup. Win probability against an opponent is the exact sum over the two run distributions (eq. 11), with ties resolved by a closed-form extra-innings recursion (eqs. 13-19) that extrapolates a one-inning distribution from the ninth root of the game distribution (following Dubner 1988). Players are ranked by the D'Esopo-Lefkowitz scoring index (expected runs/inning for nine clones of the batter), computed via the absorbing-chain fundamental matrix (I-Q)^-1. Pitching/defense are folded in by scaling each team's offensive event probabilities by the ratio of opponents' pitcher OBP-against to the league-mean pitcher OBP-against, renormalizing the out probability. Data: 1989 (and 1969) National League player statistics from Neft & Cohen (1990).

**Results.** Full enumeration of all 9! = 362,880 batting orders (about 1.3 s/lineup on a SPARCstation2; ~5.5 CPU-days per team) plus three heuristics reducing the search to 44, 140, and 987 orders, each expected to come within about one run per 162-game season of the optimal order (best of single/double-placement never off by more than .022 runs/game, average .012). For the 1989 Braves, the true best order yields 3.557 expected runs/game vs 3.260 for the worst; best-vs-worst head-to-head gives 86.5-75.5, so the batting-order effect is 5.5 wins there, and across the 1989 NL the effect is 3.2-5.5 wins with a typical value of about four wins per season (vs Freeze 1974's claim of under three). Optimal lineups scored 30-50 more runs per season than worst. Lineup findings: on over half of NL teams the slugger should not bat fourth and the pitcher should not bat last; the best hitter by scoring index should bat second on 7 of 12 teams; in worst orders the pitcher batted first on 11 of 12 teams. Season prediction: model average 3.67 runs/game vs actual 3.95 for the 1989 NL, about 7 percent low (matching D'Esopo-Lefkowitz's underprediction, attributed to conservative advancement, e.g., a runner on first not scoring on a double). Predicted vs actual 1989 wins (Table 3) were close for some teams (Giants 90.7 vs 92, Phillies 67.9 vs 67, Expos 82.6 vs 81) and far off for others (Cubs 86.9 vs 93, Astros 72.8 vs 86, Braves 79.6 vs 73). Per-inning expected runs reproduce Lindsey's (1961) high-1st/low-2nd pattern (.52/.35 model vs .53/.38 Lindsey) but miss his 3rd- and 6th-inning jumps (times-through-order and pitcher tiring/pinch-hitting effects). ### A4 — Jensen, Shirley & Wyner 2009 (AoAS)

**Citation.** Jensen, S. T., Shirley, K. E., & Wyner, A. J. (2009). "Bayesball: A Bayesian Hierarchical Model for Evaluating Fielding in Major League Baseball." Annals of Applied Statistics, 3(2), 491-520. DOI: 10.1214/08-AOAS228.

**Access used.** Full text: the complete 30-page Project Euclid PDF was downloaded and all pages extracted to text and read directly. The WebFetch intermediate summary was discarded where it conflicted with the actual text (it hallucinated logistic/thin-plate-spline details and several statistics; the real model is probit with distance/angle covariates).

**Objective.** Build a model-based fielding evaluation that treats ball-in-play (BIP) location as a continuous variable instead of the large discrete zones used by UZR/PMR/Plus-Minus. The goal is to estimate each fielder's spatial probability of converting a BIP into an out, share information across fielders at the same position via a Bayesian hierarchy, and aggregate the individual-vs-average probability differences into SAFE (Spatial Aggregate Fielding Evaluation): expected runs saved or cost per season relative to the average fielder at the position, with full posterior uncertainty.

**Method.** Data are Baseball Info Solutions (x,y) BIP locations (~4x4 ft resolution) for 2002-2005, ~120,000 BIP/year, split into flyballs (33%, incl. pop-ups), liners (25%), grounders (42%), with an ordinal 3-level human-charted velocity. For flyballs/liners, catch success is a per-player probit regression on 2D distance D from an estimated positional starting centroid, a forward/backward indicator F, velocity V, and interactions: p_ij = Phi(b_i0 + b_i1*D + b_i2*D*F + b_i3*D*V + b_i4*D*V*F). Grounders use a 1D angle-from-position version with a left/right indicator. Separate models are fit per year x BIP type x position (18 combos x 4 years = 72 fits; pitchers/catchers excluded; grounders infield-only). The hierarchy shrinks each player's 5-vector beta_i toward a position-level Normal(mu, Sigma) with diagonal Sigma and noninformative prior p(mu_k, sigma_k) proportional to 1 (Gelman 2006); an inverse-Gamma alternative changed almost nothing. Estimation is Gibbs sampling with Albert-Chib probit data augmentation. SAFE integrates f(location,v) [kernel-density BIP frequency] x r_tot(location,v) [run consequence from linear weights: single 0.5, double 0.8, triple 1.1, weighted by local hit-type frequencies] x s_pos(location,v) [shared-responsibility: relative frequency of successful plays by each position at that spot] x [p_i - p_+] (player curve minus positional average curve), computed per Gibbs draw to get a full posterior distribution of SAFE runs per player-season.

**Results.** In the CF-2005 flyball illustration (15,767 CF-eligible flyballs, N=138 CFs, n_i from 1 to 531): shrinkage was substantial for the distance and forward coefficients, smaller for velocity, minimal for the intercept; posterior means of the between-player SDs sigma_k were 0.15 (intercept), 0.28 (distance), 0.28 (velocity), 0.17 (forward), with no posterior mass near zero — so complete pooling is rejected, while no-pooling fits for low-n players were undefined or clearly unrealistic. The observed best-worst CF catch-rate gap (15.1% = Andruw Jones 74.1% vs Preston Wilson 59.0%) was far more likely under partial pooling than complete pooling. Removing scorer-assigned errors left the hierarchical curves essentially unchanged but substantially moved the no-pooling curves — shrinkage confers outlier robustness. SAFE magnitudes are smaller than UZR's (a shrinkage effect): best/worst regular player-years (>500 BIP) run roughly +14.5 (Spivey 2B 2005) to -18.5 (Jeter SS 2005; 95% interval -29.1 to -9.2); A-Rod SS 2003 was +13.5. Many best/worst posterior intervals still include zero. SAFE-UZR correlations over 423 shared players: totals 0.397 (2002), 0.440 (2003), 0.317 (2004); highest at CF (0.546-0.635), low at 3B and SS in some years. Year-to-year (2002 to 2003) reliability: SAFE 0.372 overall vs UZR 0.369; SAFE clearly better at CF (0.525 vs 0.285), LF (0.594 vs 0.548), 3B (0.503 vs 0.376), but much worse at SS (-0.030 vs 0.247); 2002-2004 correlations lower for both.

**Conclusions.** A hierarchical spatial probit with partial pooling yields smoother, less variable, outlier-robust fielder evaluations than zone tabulation, is competitive with UZR overall, and has superior year-to-year consistency in the outfield — but inconsistent infield (especially SS) behavior, ignored park geometry, and an assumed-constant ability across years are acknowledged limitations. Transfer to this MLB game-prediction project: the paper validates the exact shrinkage machinery (estimate between-unit variance from data, shrink low-sample units toward the group mean, weight by event frequency and run consequence) that B9a/B9b defensive features need, though it evaluates players retrospectively and offers no direct evidence on pregame game-outcome predictive value.

**Proposed experiments:**
- **B9a-shrunk: team defensive out-conversion with data-estimated shrinkage weight** (→ B9a; metric: Totals MAE (vs 3.533 baseline) and walk-forward win accuracy/AUC; secondary: Poisson deviance of runs heads)
  Build flag-gated team-defense columns (BABIP-against, out-conversion rate on BIP, optionally xHits-saved from Statcast xBA) as empirical-Bayes shrunken rates: W = tau^2/(tau^2 + sigma^2/n_BIP) with tau^2 estimated from observed between-team variance minus sampling variance each season, per the paper's core lesson that the shrinkage variance must be estimated from the data (their sigma_k posteriors excluded zero — heterogeneity is real, so W must not be hard-coded or allowed to collapse to complete pooling). ### A5 — Deshpande & Wyner 2017 (JQAS)

**Citation.** Deshpande, S.K. & Wyner, A.J. (2017). "A hierarchical Bayesian model of pitch framing." Journal of Quantitative Analysis in Sports 13(3): 95-112. Open-access preprint: arXiv:1704.00823v2 [stat.AP], 9 Sep 2017.

**Access used.** Full text: arXiv PDF (1704.00823v2, 36 pp) fetched and text-extracted locally; all numbers verified against the extracted text. The intermediate WebFetch auto-summary was discarded as unreliable (it contained figures not present in the paper).

**Objective.** Estimate each MLB home-plate umpire's probability of calling a strike, adjusting for pitch location, count, and the pitch participants (batter, catcher, pitcher), and from that isolate each catcher's "framing" effect on called-strike probability over and above those confounders. Translate framing effects into average runs saved per season with full posterior uncertainty quantification (absent in prior framing studies), and introduce CAFE, a confounder-free framing metric that integrates each catcher's effect over the league-wide pitch distribution.

**Method.** Data are PITCHf/x taken pitches 2011-2015; the main model is fit on 2014 (N=308,388 taken pitches within one foot of an approximate rule-book strike zone; 93 umpires, 101 catchers, 719 pitchers, 1010 batters). Pitch location enters via "historical GAMs": logistic GAM smooths of called-strike log-odds fit on 2011-2013 data, one per batter-pitcher handedness combination, whose forecast log-odds become a continuous covariate. The core model is a separate Bayesian logistic regression per umpire: log-odds(strike) = umpire-specific intercept and location-slope terms plus count, catcher, pitcher, and batter partial effects, with hierarchical Gaussian priors shrinking each umpire's parameters toward the league mean (umpire-to-umpire variance hyperparameters fixed at tau^2=0.25; Inverse-Gamma(3,3) hyperpriors on top-level variances; baseline catcher Brayan Pena, baseline count 0-0). Five nested models of increasing complexity are compared: Model 1 (umpire-specific location only), Model 2 (+ shared count and catcher effects), Model 3 (+ shared pitcher and batter effects), Models 4-5 (umpire-specific count/catcher and player effects; up to 171,168 parameters). All fit by MCMC in Stan (R-hat < 1.1, ESS > 1000). Framing impact = posterior difference in called-strike probability vs the baseline catcher, weighted by a count-specific run value rho(count) of a called strike computed from 2011-2014 run expectancy; summed over a catcher's pitches (RS) or integrated over the empirical league pitch distribution and rescaled to 4000 pitches (CAFE).

**Results.** Model 3 (umpire-specific location surfaces, but count/catcher/pitcher/batter effects constant across umpires) wins out-of-sample on 2015 data (overall misclassification 0.105 vs 0.109 for the historical GAM; borderline "Region 1" 0.244 vs 0.267) and in 10-fold CV; the umpire-interaction Models 4-5 overfit (best in-sample, worse out-of-sample). Umpire heterogeneity is real: e.g. Angel Hernandez's 50% called-strike contour on a 2-0 count covers 4.37 sq ft vs the average umpire's 3.87 and Mike DiMuro's 3.53; empirically Hernandez called 42.67% of taken pitches strikes vs DiMuro's 39.92%. All catcher effects on the log-odds scale lie in [-1.5, 1.5], with the maximum catcher-to-catcher difference about 3 log-odds — comparable in magnitude to count effects. The count-specific run value of a called strike ranges from 0.062 (0-0) to 0.540 (3-2), weighted average 0.11 runs. 2014 RS leaders: Miguel Montero 25.71 runs saved (SD 5.03, 95% CI [15.61, 35.09]) relative to the baseline catcher; worst Jarrod Saltalamacchia -23.98 [-32.76, -15.87]; correlation with Baseball Prospectus (Judge et al. 2015) estimates is 0.94. On the opportunity-adjusted CAFE scale (per 4000 average pitches), Hank Conger leads at 16.20 (SD 2.72) and Tomas Telis trails at -25.06. Credible intervals are wide: the model separates good framers from bad ones but cannot rank within those groups (Conger's 95% rank interval is [1, 11]). Year-to-year CAFE correlations for 56 catchers appearing 2012-2015 are 0.70 (2012-13), 0.71 (2013-14), 0.58 (2014-15) — on par with slugging percentage.

**Conclusions.** Some catchers exert a genuine, substantial effect on umpires' called-strike probability, roughly as large as count effects, and framing is moderately reliable year to year (r ~ 0.6-0.7), but posterior uncertainty is large enough that a single season cannot precisely value an individual catcher (Lucroy's RS CI spans 8.16-30.49 runs, i.e. ~$5.7M-$21.3M), and umpire-specific catcher/player interactions cannot be reliably estimated from one season — hierarchical shrinkage toward the league mean is essential. Transferability to this project: the paper directly validates modeling per-umpire call tendencies as shrunken deviations from a league-average surface (supporting a shrunken UMP K-factor) and gives realistic effect sizes and reliabilities for future catcher-framing features, though the 2014 PITCHf/x era predates modern receiving styles and any ABS/challenge-era changes, so magnitudes should be re-estimated on current Statcast data.

**Proposed experiments:**
- **UMP-1: Shrunken per-umpire K/called-strike factor via predicted HP umpire** (→ UMP pregame umpire serve path (crew-rotation-predicted HP ump K factor); metric: Totals MAE vs 3.533 baseline (primary; umpire zone size moves run environment) and win accuracy/AUC vs .561/.581; per-PA model K-class log-loss if fed to the batter model.)
  Build a point-in-time per-umpire feature: empirical-Bayes/hierarchically shrunken deviation of each umpire's called-strike rate (and resulting K rate and runs-per-game allowed) from league average, computed from all games with data_through_date before the target game, joined via the crew-rotation-predicted home-plate umpire. The paper justifies exactly this shape: umpire intercept/location heterogeneity is real and estimable with shrinkage (its prior, tau^2=0.25 on log-odds, implies <10% prior chance two umpires split 75%/25% on the same borderline pitch — a useful scale for how hard to shrink), while umpire-x-catcher or umpire-x-player interactions overfit a season of data and should NOT be attempted. ### P1 — The Book (Tango/Lichtman/Dolphin 2007)

**Citation.** Tom M. Tango, Mitchel G. Lichtman & Andrew E. Dolphin (2007), "The Book: Playing the Percentages in Baseball", Potomac Books, Washington D.C., 385 pp., ISBN 9781597971294.

**Access used.** Full text NOT read (archive.org record is print-restricted, metadata only). Summary assembled from: the archive.org catalog record; tangotiger.net wiki Log5 page (formulas); FanGraphs "Estimating Hitter Platoon Skill" (Klaassen), which quotes The Book's platoon regression PA amounts; Lichtman's Baseball Prospectus article "Everything You Always Wanted to Know About the Times Through the Order Penalty" (author of The Book, 2000-2012 update); RotoWire "The Saber's Edge: Batter vs. Pitcher Stats" (BvP quote); plus web-search summaries. Grokipedia page returned 403 and was not used.

**Objective.** The book set out to test common baseball strategy and roster-usage beliefs (platooning, lineup construction, reliever usage, sacrifice bunts, intentional walks, batter-vs-pitcher "ownership") with rigorous empirical methods rather than folklore. It builds a common currency (wOBA, a linear-weights on-base-style rate stat) and a matchup framework (the odds-ratio / log5 method) so that any batter-pitcher-league combination can be projected, and quantifies how much observed splits must be regressed to the mean before they can be trusted as skill.

**Method.** The authors use play-by-play data (roughly the 1999-2006 era) aggregated into wOBA-denominated event rates, Markov-chain run-expectancy and win-expectancy tables by base-out state, and regression-to-the-mean estimation of how much of an observed split is skill vs noise. The core matchup tool is the odds-ratio method (equivalent to Bill James' log5, derivable from Bayes): for a team matchup W%(A v B) = W%(A)(1-W%(B)) / [W%(A)(1-W%(B)) + (1-W%(A))W%(B)], with a home-advantage odds multiplier variant (HA about .54 for MLB); for batter/pitcher event rates the same odds-ratio combination of batter, pitcher, and league-average odds gives the expected matchup rate. Skill-vs-noise questions are answered by finding the number of PA of league-average performance to mix with an observed split so the blended estimate is unbiased (an empirical-Bayes shrinkage constant). Leverage of game situations is quantified via win-expectancy swings (the Leverage Index concept associated with Tango), used to evaluate reliever deployment. Times-through-the-order effects are measured by splitting starter performance by how many times the batting order has cycled.

**Results.** Verified quantitative findings from secondary treatments citing the book: platoon splits must be heavily regressed — left-handed hitters' platoon skill is regressed against about 1000 PA vs LHP of league-average split, right-handed hitters against about 2200 PA vs LHP, switch-hitters stabilize around 600 career PA vs LHP; for pitchers, roughly 450 BFP vs LHP (lefty pitchers) and 700 (righty pitchers). Lefty hitters show more true variation in platoon skill than righties, hence less regression. Times-through-the-order penalty: starter wOBA-against rises about 9 points from first to second time through the order and about 8 points from second to third, attributed to batter familiarity rather than fatigue (Lichtman's 2013 BP follow-up on 2000-2012 data: .340/.350/.359/.359 by TTO, roughly .35 runs per 9 worse by the third pass, with pitch count having little effect). Batter-vs-pitcher career matchups: no predictive value beyond platoon effects — "given the choice between that player's 1,500 PA over the past three years against the rest of the league or twenty-five PA against that particular opponent, look at the 1,500 PA." The book also concludes the best reliever should be used in the highest-leverage situations regardless of inning, and that the sacrifice bunt and intentional walk are usually run-expectancy losers (qualitative here; specific tables not verifiable without full text).

**Conclusions.** The authors conclude that most matchup information is best obtained by combining each player's regressed overall rates through the odds-ratio method against a league baseline, that small-sample splits (platoon, BvP, clutch) are dominated by noise and must be shrunk with large sample-size-denominated constants, and that within-game pitcher decline is driven by batter familiarity (times through the order), not pitch count. This is MLB-native research, so it transfers directly; the main transfer risk is era drift (2000s run environment and starter-usage patterns differ from 2026).

**Proposed experiments:**
- **TTO-aware per-PA feature and SP-mixture weighting** (→ NEW (per-PA SP-mixture weights, per reading notes); metric: Per-PA multinomial log-loss; batter stat-line MAE; totals MAE)
  Add a point-in-time times-through-order index (1st/2nd/3rd pass) as a feature to the per-PA 8-class multinomial, and set the SP-vs-bullpen mixture weights in the analytic stat-line aggregation from the starter's expected TTO exposure (expected BF distribution) instead of a flat share, encoding the ~8-9 wOBA-point-per-pass penalty. ### P2 — Marcel projection system (Tango 2004)

**Citation.** Tango, T. "Marcel The Monkey Forecasting System." tangotiger.net, original description 2004 (tangotiger.net/archives/stud0346.shtml), with annual forecast pages through ~2012 at tangotiger.net/marcel/. Self-published sabermetrics resource, not a peer-reviewed venue.

**Access used.** Web pages: tangotiger.net/marcel/ (index/overview) and tangotiger.net/archives/stud0346.shtml (original 2004 recipe, fetched in full). Performance-vs-other-systems claims supplemented by secondary treatments found via web search: Beyond the Box Score projection reviews (2015 and 2016 seasons) and the FanGraphs Library projection rundown; the BtBS article bodies could not be fetched (connection errors), so their findings are cited from search-result summaries only.

**Objective.** Marcel is a deliberately minimal MLB player projection system intended as a competence floor: "the most basic forecasting system you can have, that uses as little intelligence as possible." Tango's stated purpose is not to stand behind the forecasts but to establish the minimum accuracy any serious forecaster must beat, using only weighted history, regression to the mean, and a simple age adjustment. It projects roughly 1,800 players per season.

**Method.** The recipe, per the original 2004 description: (1) take a player's last three MLB seasons of rate stats, weighted 5/4/3 with the most recent season weighted 5; (2) regress toward league average by adding a ballast of 1,200 PA of league-average performance into the weighted mixture (equivalently, reliability = weighted PA / (weighted PA + 1200), so low-sample players collapse toward the league mean); (3) apply a linear age adjustment centered at age 29 — a multiplicative bump of (29 − age) × 0.006 per year for players under 29 (improvement) and (29 − age) × 0.003 for players over 29 (decline); the original page notes a sign bug that was corrected to (29 − age). (4) Playing time is projected as 0.5 × previous-season PA + 0.1 × PA two seasons back + 200. There are no park adjustments, no minor-league data, no injury or profile modeling — the omissions are intentional. It is a pure shrinkage-plus-decay estimator, which is exactly the machinery relevant to this project's early-season blend problem.

**Results.** The Marcel page itself makes only a qualitative claim: compared against leading forecasting systems, "Ain't much difference." Secondary evaluations support this: Beyond the Box Score's reviews of the 2015 and 2016 projection seasons found that only Steamer was consistently more accurate than Marcel, that Marcel was not substantially worse than the mainstream systems for virtually every stat and player subset, and that Marcel beat at least one of PECOTA or ZiPS in every category examined; in the 2015 hitter review Marcel and Steamer had the best results. I could not retrieve the underlying RMSE/correlation tables (the BtBS pages failed to load), so no specific error numbers are reported here — the documented finding is directional: decades of head-to-head evaluations show sophisticated systems add only small accuracy gains over the 5/4/3 + 1200-PA-ballast recipe.

**Conclusions.** Tango's conclusion is that most of the signal in baseball forecasting is captured by three ingredients — recency-weighted multi-year history, regression to the mean proportional to sample size, and a coarse age curve — and anything a real system adds beyond that is marginal. Transferability to this MLB project is direct for the shrinkage/decay machinery (team-level rate priors and per-PA batter rates are the same estimation problem), though Marcel is a player-season projection system, so its specific constants (5/4/3, 1200 PA, age-29 pivot) are starting anchors to tune, not values to import verbatim at the team level.

### P3 — FiveThirtyEight mlb-elo methodology (GitHub)

**Citation.** Jay Boice (model creator; site by FiveThirtyEight/Nate Silver's team), "How Our MLB Predictions Work," FiveThirtyEight Methodology page, first published April 2016, last edited May 2, 2022 (model v1.5); canonical surviving companion: fivethirtyeight/data repository, mlb-elo dataset README (https://github.com/fivethirtyeight/data/blob/master/mlb-elo/README.md).

**Access used.** Full text of both sources: the mlb-elo dataset README via raw.githubusercontent.com (WebFetch summary of full content including complete column list), and the complete archived methodology article "How Our MLB Predictions Work" (May 2, 2022 edit, web.archive.org capture) downloaded via curl and read in full as extracted text.

**Objective.** Document the methodology behind FiveThirtyEight's public MLB game and season forecasts: an Elo-based team rating system covering 1871-2023 that produces pregame win probabilities, playoff odds, and World Series odds. The system's stated design goal is a simple, tunable competitor-rating framework extended with baseball-specific adjustments — home field, margin of victory, park/era effects, travel, rest, and (most importantly, per the authors) starting pitchers. The README additionally documents the released game-by-game dataset (mlb_elo.csv, mlb_elo_latest.csv) containing both a simple Elo and the full "rating" system with pitcher columns.

**Method.** Each team carries an Elo-style rating (mean ~1500) updated after every game by an amount depending on pregame win probability and margin of victory. Pregame adjustments: home-field advantage = 24 rating points (reduced to 9.6 for fanless games, July 2020 tweak); travel penalty = miles_traveled^(1/3) * -0.31 (up to ~4 points); rest = 2.3 points per day of rest, capped at three days. Starting pitchers are handled via a Tangotiger-modified Bill James game score, gameScore = 47.4 + K + 1.5*outs - 2*BB - 2*H - 3*R - 4*HR, normalized for era and park and adjusted for opposing-team offensive strength; each start feeds a pitcher's rolling game score (rGS), with debut pitchers initialized below average but updated faster per start, and each team also carries a team-level rGS over all its starters. The pitcher's pregame team-rating adjustment is ratingAdj = 4.7 * (pitcher rGS - team rGS) — i.e., a delta versus the team's own rotation baseline, not an absolute pitcher quality term. From 2022, projected "openers" (identified by a three-condition appearance-history rule) get the team-average adjustment instead, and their game scores are excluded from rGS updates. Preseason ratings blend 67% from preseason win projections from three external systems (PECOTA, FanGraphs depth charts, Clay Davenport) scaled to Elo range, and 33% from the team's prior-season final rating reverted one-third toward the mean; preseason team rGS is the start-weighted average of individual pitcher rGSs using FanGraphs projected starts. Season outcomes come from "hot" Monte Carlo simulations (ratings update within simulated seasons); since 2019, ratings change at three-quarters of their previous speed to reduce forecast variance. A separate simplified Elo (no pitchers/travel/rest, pure one-third mean reversion between seasons) powers the historical "Complete History of MLB" series.

**Results.** This is a methodology article, not a paper, so reported results are sparse and self-reported without significance testing. The one headline evaluation: adding the starting-pitcher adjustment improved the share of games correctly "called" by about 1 percentage point, with a "corresponding improvement" in mean squared error of game-by-game forecasts (no baseline numbers given). Illustrative magnitude: June-2000 Pedro Martinez was worth ~109 rating points per start, equivalent to roughly a 15-percentage-point boost to Boston's win probability. Concrete fitted parameters are disclosed (HFA 24 pts; fanless 9.6; travel -0.31*miles^(1/3); rest 2.3 pts/day capped at 3; pitcher adjustment coefficient 4.7; game-score constant 47.4; preseason 67/33 blend with 1/3 mean reversion; 2019 rating-speed reduction to 3/4). The exact Elo K factor and the margin-of-victory multiplier formula are NOT given in the article or README, so I omit them. No out-of-sample accuracy, calibration, or market-comparison numbers are published in these sources.

**Conclusions.** The authors' effective conclusions: a lightly-adjusted Elo captures MLB team strength well; starting pitchers are the single most valuable pregame adjustment (worth ~1pp of game-call accuracy) and are best expressed as a pitcher-vs-own-rotation delta on an opponent/park/era-adjusted rolling game score; and preseason priors are much better built from projection systems (67%) than from carried-over ratings alone (33%, further reverted 1/3 to mean). This is MLB-native work, so transferability is direct — the main translation risk is that all parameters are on Elo-rating scale and tuned for a rating system, whereas this project's LightGBM heads need the underlying quantities re-expressed as features with coefficients learned rather than borrowed.

### P4 — Baseball Savant OAA methodology

**Citation.** MLB Statcast / Baseball Savant, "Outs Above Average" leaderboard (https://baseballsavant.mlb.com/leaderboard/outs_above_average, data 2016-2026); methodology per MLB.com Statcast Glossary "Outs Above Average (OAA)" (Petriello/MLB.com) and Tom Tango, "Introducing Infield Outs Above Average," MLB Technology Blog (Medium), Jan 2020.

**Access used.** web_page — leaderboard page text via WebFetch plus direct curl downloads of five CSV exports (season, range=5, range=6, team-level) to verify columns and month filtering numerically; full text of Tango's MLB Technology Blog infield-OAA article; the MLB.com glossary page itself returned HTTP 406, so its methodology points come from the leaderboard's own glossary text and search-result excerpts of the glossary (FanGraphs/Beyond the Box Score as secondary treatments).

**Objective.** Baseball Savant's OAA leaderboard publishes a range-based fielding-skill metric: the cumulative sum of per-play credits/debits for every fielding opportunity, quantifying how many outs a fielder (or team) has saved versus expectation given play difficulty. It also publishes a position-based translation of outs saved into runs saved ("Fielding Runs Prevented," player-level CSV column fielding_runs_prevented). The purpose is descriptive defensive evaluation, but the downloadable data is directly usable as a team-defense feature source.

**Method.** Outfield OAA is built on Catch Probability: each batted ball gets a catch likelihood from the distance the fielder must cover, the opportunity time (clock starts at pitcher's release), and the direction traveled. Infield OAA (introduced 2020) uses a distance-time-intercept model with four primary inputs: how far the fielder must go to reach the ball, time available, distance/proximity to the base, and the batter-runner's speed — using the runner's seasonal-average Sprint Speed rather than that play's speed, with a league-average 27 ft/sec default for players without data; Tango's model then adds throw time (~0.75s release + ~1s ball flight) and calls the runner safe if throw time exceeds run time. Per-play accounting: a converted out credits (1 − out probability), a missed play debits (0 − out probability); e.g., a 60% play is +0.40 if made, −0.60 if missed. OAA is the season sum of these; Runs Prevented converts outs saved to runs saved "using position-based calculations" (exact coefficients not published on the pages read). Leaderboard filters: years 2016-2026, split-years, position (IF/OF/1B/2B/3B/SS/LF/CF/RF), team, view type (Fielder, Fielding Team, Batter, Batting Team, Pitcher), minimum-attempts qualifier, and a month filter (Year / March-April / May / June / July / August / September).

**Results.** This is a data product, not a study, so the key "results" are coverage and export mechanics, which I verified by direct download. (1) CSV export works unauthenticated via &csv=true; player-level columns: player_id, team, position, fielding_runs_prevented, outs_above_average, directional OAA splits (in-front/lateral-3B/lateral-1B/behind), RHH/LHH splits, actual vs adjusted-estimated success rate and their difference. (3) Team-level export (type=Fielding_Team) works but the returned team CSV had OAA and splits without a fielding_runs_prevented column. (4) The year column is empty in exports — season must be tracked from request parameters. (5) No day-level or arbitrary date-range filter exists; month buckets are the finest grain. (6) Model calibration (Tango, infield, 2019): plays with out probability 20-79% were estimated to convert 61% of the time and actually converted 64%.

**Conclusions.** MLB's stated conclusion is that OAA is a properly difficulty-adjusted, range-based measure of fielding skill covering both outfield (2016+) and infield (2020+), with reasonable calibration of the underlying out-probability model, and that Runs Prevented expresses it in run units. ### P5 — UmpScorecards methodology

**Citation.** Ethan Singer et al., "UmpScorecards — Methodology Explainers: Accuracy & Expected Stats; Consistency; Favor; True Strike Zone; Estimated Umpire Zone; Automated Balls and Strikes (ABS)," umpscorecards.com/page/info/explainers/*, accessed 2026-07-22 (living web resource, updated for the 2026 ABS-challenge era).

**Access used.** Full text of six methodology web pages (accuracy, consistency, favor, true-strike-zone, estimated-umpire-zone, abs explainers) via WebFetch, plus WebSearch secondary sources (CBS Sports, ESPN, Baseball-Reference ABS pages) for 2026 challenge-rule details and the ~94% league accuracy figure attributed to UmpScorecards.

**Objective.** UmpScorecards builds per-game, per-umpire measurement of home-plate umpire performance: accuracy (share of taken pitches called correctly), consistency (agreement with the umpire's own implied zone), and favor (net run expectancy handed to each team by missed calls). The explainers document exactly how each metric is computed from Hawk-Eye/Statcast pitch tracking, and — critically for 2026+ — how the methodology was rebuilt around MLB's ABS challenge-system strike zone, which became the operative definition of a strike this season.

**Method.** Pre-2026 accuracy: the true zone is the rulebook zone — 17 inches wide, with per-pitch top/bottom set by a Statcast operator (values MLB reports on every pitch), using collision geometry including the ball's radius. Hawk-Eye measurement error is handled by Monte Carlo: 500 simulated true locations per pitch from an error distribution; a call is an error only if the opposite call had >90% probability (near-certain misses only). Expected accuracy is a gradient-boosted classifier trained on the prior full season (per-pitch call difficulty) with a beta-calibration layer refit on rolling 30-day windows, yielding accuracy-above-expected. Consistency compares calls against an Estimated Umpire Zone built per game via kernel density estimation: KDE over all taken pitches and over called strikes, combined with Bayes' theorem into prob_strike(x,z); the EUZ is the contiguous 50% contour (this replaced an outlier-prone convex-hull approach). Favor converts each missed call into a run-expectancy delta over 288 states (8 base states x 3 outs x 12 counts, values from the prior 5 MLB seasons) and sums per team. From 2026, the site instead adopts the official ABS zone — 17 inches wide, top at 53.5% and bottom at 27% of batter height, evaluated at the center of home plate (8.5 in behind the front edge) with no margin of error or Monte Carlo tolerance — because challenges overturn umpires against exactly that zone; scorecards now also track challenge frequency, overturn rates, and favor both before and after challenge corrections.

**Results.** These are methodology pages, not a study, so quantitative content is validation- and magnitude-oriented. Their ABS-zone implementation agreed with official ABS rulings on over 99.5% of roughly 1,600 challenged pitches in spring training 2025-26. Applying the strict ABS zone instead of the tolerance-based method raises measured error counts by about 1.5 pitches per 100 and lowers league-wide accuracy by roughly 1-2 percentage points, so pre-2026 and 2026+ accuracy figures are explicitly not comparable (pre-2026 data is left under the old method). Secondary coverage citing UmpScorecards puts league-average umpire accuracy near 94% of taken pitches. The favor explainer illustrates scale with a bases-loaded, two-out, 3-2 example where one missed call swings expectancy by about 1.74 runs; it acknowledges favor ignores downstream sequencing effects. The pages I could read do not publish a single number for umpire-to-umpire spread in accuracy or favor, so I omit that magnitude. The 2026 challenge rules (per secondary sources: two challenges per team, retained when successful, initiated immediately by batter/pitcher/catcher) cap in-game corrections at a handful of pitches, meaning umpire zone tendencies still govern the large majority of taken pitches even in 2026.

**Conclusions.** The authors conclude that per-umpire accuracy, consistency, and favor are measurable game-by-game with high fidelity, and that from 2026 the ABS zone is the correct ground truth because it is what actually overturns calls. This is directly an MLB source, so transferability is not an issue; the live question for this project is that 2026 creates a structural break — per-umpire K/BB effects persist (only ~2+ challenges/team/game get corrected, and highest-leverage misses are the ones overturned), but their tails are clipped and historical per-ump stats sit on a different measurement basis than 2026+ stats.

### R-A2 — Karlis & Ntzoufras 2003 (JRSS-D)

**Citation.** Karlis, D. & Ntzoufras, I. (2003). "Analysis of sports data by using bivariate Poisson models." Journal of the Royal Statistical Society Series D (The Statistician), 52(3), 381-393.

**Access used.** Full text read. WebFetch failed (the AUEB mirror is HTTP-only and refused the forced HTTPS upgrade), so the open author PDF was downloaded via curl over HTTP and all 13 pages were extracted with PyMuPDF; all numbers above come from the paper's text and Tables 1-6. Local copies: C:\Users\Kai\AppData\Local\Temp\claude\E--Kai-PyCharm-Projects-MLBStatsProject\eda06883-adad-492f-82ed-6c9649eb2dcb\scratchpad\karlis2003.pdf and karlis2003.txt.

**Objective.** Replace the standard independence assumption between the two teams' scores in Poisson score models with a bivariate Poisson (BP) model and extensions allowing correlation, which the authors argue is plausible because the two teams interact and share game conditions (speed of play, weather, stadium). Quantify what even slight correlation does to fit and to the distribution of draws, and propose a diagonal-inflated extension that additionally handles excess draws and overdispersed marginals. Illustrate on football (Italian Serie A 1991-92, Champions League 2000-01) and water polo (1999 European national cup).

**Method.** The BP(lambda1, lambda2, lambda3) distribution is built by trivariate reduction: X = X1+X3, Y = X2+X3 with X_k independent Poisson(lambda_k), giving Poisson marginals with E(X)=lambda1+lambda3, E(Y)=lambda2+lambda3 and cov(X,Y)=lambda3; lambda1/lambda2 are interpreted as "net" scoring ability and lambda3 as shared game conditions. Their key analytic result: the difference Z = X-Y is free of lambda3 — Z ~ PD(lambda1, lambda2), the same Skellam/Poisson-difference distribution as for two independent Poissons — but if the independent (double-Poisson) model is fitted, the marginally estimated rates confound lambda3, so one effectively uses PD(lambda1+lambda3, lambda2+lambda3) instead of the correct PD(lambda1, lambda2). They quantify this misspecification numerically for the draw probability at football-realistic covariances (lambda3 = 0.05-0.20; observed football covariance is about 0.10). Regression structure puts log-linear predictors (home effect, attack/defence parameters) on lambda1 and lambda2, and constant or team-dependent predictors on lambda3; estimation is maximum likelihood via an EM algorithm exploiting the latent trivariate reduction. The diagonal-inflated extension is the mixture P(x,y) = (1-p)BP for x != y and (1-p)BP + p*D(x, theta) on the diagonal x = y, with D chosen as Poisson, geometric, or a discrete distribution on {0..J}; its marginals are no longer Poisson (they are overdispersed mixtures), and even with lambda3 = 0 diagonal inflation induces dependence. Models are compared by LRT, AIC and BIC, including against the saturated model.

**Results.** Misspecification effect: with lambda1 = lambda2 = 1, a true BP with lambda3 = 0.05 implies about 3.3% more draws than the independent-Poisson fit predicts, rising to 14% more at lambda3 = 0.20 — explaining the common empirical underestimation of draws. Serie A 1991-92 (Table 1): double Poisson log-lik -771.5 (AIC 1614.9); BP with constant lambda3 -764.9 (AIC 1603.9; LRT for lambda3 = 0, p < 0.01); best model is the diagonal-inflated BP with a Discrete(1) (Bernoulli at 1, i.e., 1-1 score) inflation: log-lik -756.6, 39 parameters, AIC 1591.1, BIC 1763.7, saturated-model comparison p = 0.85. Estimated parameters for the selected structure: lambda3 = 0.23, mixing proportion p = 0.09, theta1 = 1.00; observed 1-1 draws were 58 vs 33 predicted by double Poisson, and the inflated model reproduces 58 exactly while plain BP overestimates 0-0 (49 vs 38 observed) because it lowers net team rates. Champions League 2000-01: best model is BP with constant lambda3 (LRT p = 0.042); inflation did not help because draws were not underestimated. Water polo 1999 (12 teams, 50 games, mean scores near 8): BP with constant lambda3 dominates (log-lik -171.91 vs -186.67 double Poisson; AIC 391.8 vs 417.3), with lambda3 = 5.55 (5.50 in the Hungary-baseline variant) — a large share of the marginal mean — and team-dependent lambda3 was not supported (p = 0.602). NBA/Euroleague score correlations quoted in passing: 0.41 and 0.38 (2000-01).

**Conclusions.** Authors' conclusions: BP models improve fit and draw prediction over independent Poisson at negligible parameter cost; diagonal inflation further handles both excess draws and overdispersion; the EM algorithm makes these practical. The paper's transferable core for a non-soccer setting is analytic, not empirical: (1) the score DIFFERENCE is Skellam(lambda1, lambda2) regardless of covariance, so an independent-heads Skellam functional form for the MARGIN is structurally justified — BUT heads trained to match marginal run rates estimate lambda1+lambda3 and lambda2+lambda3, so the implied margin variance m1+m2 overstates the true lambda1+lambda2 = m1+m2-2*lambda3 whenever runs are positively correlated, shrinking win probabilities toward 0.5; (2) symmetrically, cov(X,Y)=lambda3 means the TOTAL X+Y has variance m1+m2+2*lambda3, so the independent model understates totals dispersion (this variance identity is an immediate consequence of the paper's stated moments; the paper itself demonstrates the effect via draw probabilities, not totals). Transferability caveats: results are from soccer (means ~1-1.5, covariance ~0.1-0.23) and water polo (means ~8, lambda3 ~5.5); MLB has no draws (diagonal inflation per se is irrelevant), run scoring is overdispersed relative to Poisson (bursty innings), and walk-off/home-half-ninth truncation distorts the margin distribution near +-1 in ways this paper does not address. Fit comparison is in-sample (AIC/BIC/LRT); no out-of-sample prediction or betting evaluation is performed.

**Proposed experiments:**
- **E12-cov: covariance-corrected Skellam win probability** (→ E12 (Skellam / dispersion-corrected win prob), feeding E11 (the corrected sigma is a natural out-of-sample sigma variant for the recalibration stack); metric: Walk-forward win-prob log-loss, AUC, and reliability curve vs current .581 AUC baseline and vs market .5685 favorite accuracy)
  Estimate lambda3 as the walk-forward (point-in-time) empirical covariance of home/away run residuals (observed runs minus the two Poisson-head predictions m1, m2), pooled or conditioned on park/roof/temperature. Compute win prob from Skellam/normal margin with mean m1-m2 and variance m1+m2-2*lambda3_hat (falling back to lambda3=0 replicates the naive independent Skellam).   MLB runs are overdispersed vs Poisson (the paper's motivation for mixture/inflated marginals). ### R-A4 — Glickman & Stern 1998 (JASA)

**Citation.** Glickman, M. E., & Stern, H. S. (1998). "A State-Space Model for National Football League Scores." Journal of the American Statistical Association, 93(441), 25-35. https://www.glicko.net/research/nfl.pdf

**Access used.** Full text read. WebFetch of the verified URL returned the raw PDF binary (1.5 MB, 11 pages), which was saved locally; text was extracted with pypdf from the project venv and all 11 pages were read directly. All numbers quoted come from the paper's own tables (Tables 2-4) and text.

**Objective.** Build a predictive model for NFL game score differences in which team strengths are explicitly time-varying, separating two sources of strength change: short-term week-to-week variation (injuries, random factors) and larger season-to-season variation (personnel/coaching turnover), plus per-team home-field advantage. The goals are plausible inference on team strengths and evolution parameters, and prediction of future game outcomes, benchmarked against the Las Vegas point spread.

**Method.** Normal linear state-space model on score differences: for a game between teams i and i' at i's home, mean = theta_i - theta_i' + alpha_i, with observation variance tau^2 common to all teams/seasons. Team strengths follow a first-order autoregressive process with SEPARATE evolution steps: between weeks, theta evolves via regression parameter beta_w (shrinkage toward the mean-centered strengths) with evolution SD sigma_w; between seasons via beta_s with SD sigma_s. The centering matrix G makes shrinkage act around the (drifting) league mean, since only strength differences are identified. Home-field advantage is a separate per-team, time-invariant parameter alpha_i (prior mean 3 points). Fully Bayesian estimation by Gibbs sampling (Kalman-filter forward pass + back-filtering for the strength sequence): 7 parallel chains of 18,000 iterations with overdispersed starts, convergence checked by potential scale reduction, final sample of 7,000 draws. Data: 1988-1993 regular seasons, 28 teams, 16 games/team/season; the model was fit through week 10 of 1993 and the remaining 110 games of 1993 were predicted, refitting weekly (5,000-iteration single-chain updates) and comparing point predictions to the Vegas line. Posterior predictive discrepancy diagnostics checked homogeneity of the regression variance across seasons and teams and the home-field structure.

**Results.** Posterior means (95% intervals), Table 3: observation SD tau = 12.78 points (12.23, 13.35); week-to-week evolution SD sigma_w = 0.88 (0.52, 1.36); season-to-season evolution SD sigma_s = 2.35 (1.14, 3.87); week-to-week regression beta_w = 0.99 (0.96, 1.02), i.e. essentially no within-season shrinkage; between-season regression beta_s = 0.82 (0.52, 1.28), i.e. roughly 18% regression of (mean-centered) strength toward the league mean per offseason, though with a wide interval; the joint posterior of (sigma_w, sigma_s) is bimodal with modes near (0.6, 3) and (0.9, 2). HFA parameters center around 3.2 points, ranging from about 1.6 (Cowboys, Browns) to 7.28 (Oilers); HFA SD sigma_h = 2.28; a diagnostic on an earlier single-HFA model showed lack of fit (tail probability .05), motivating per-team HFA. Team strengths after week 10 of 1993 ranged from +9.06 (Dallas) to -7.73 (New England), about a 17-point best-to-worst gap. Out-of-sample on the last 110 games of 1993: model MSE 165.0 vs point spread 170.5; MAE 10.50 vs 10.84; winners correct 64/110 (58.2%) vs 63/110 for the line; 65/110 model predictions were on the correct side of the point spread. The authors explicitly state the sample is too small for the edge over the spread to generalize.

**Conclusions.** The authors conclude the state-space model captures the main components of variability in football outcomes and predicts about as well as the Las Vegas line ("performs at least as well"), i.e. it tracks team strength comparably to expert opinion, without claiming a proven market edge. Key structural findings: season-to-season strength innovation (sigma_s ~ 2.35) is materially larger than week-to-week innovation (sigma_w ~ 0.88); between-season shrinkage toward the mean is real (beta_s ~ 0.82) while within-season shrinkage is absent (beta_w ~ 1, could be fixed at 1); per-team HFA is needed. Transferability note: this is NFL (16-game seasons, 28 teams, normal model on point differences), not MLB. The qualitative structure — persist ~most of centered strength across an offseason, add an offseason innovation variance several times the weekly innovation, and let early-season data update a shrunk prior via the implied Kalman gain — transfers well to MLB Elo/team-rate priors, but the specific magnitudes (0.82 carryover, point-scale SDs, 3.2-point HFA) must be re-estimated on MLB data; MLB's 162-game season and different roster-churn dynamics will shift them.

**Proposed experiments:**
- **Elo season-boundary carryover sweep (Glickman-Stern beta_s analogue)** (→ E9d; metric: March-June win accuracy and AUC of the Elo head (plus full-season log-loss); accept if early-season gap vs closing-line favorite (.5685) narrows significantly without hurting Aug-Oct.)
  At each season boundary, transform ratings as R_new = mean + lambda*(R_old - mean); grid lambda in {0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0} crossed with a K-inflation schedule for the first N games (K multiplier decaying to 1, N in {10, 20, 30}) to mimic the paper's offseason innovation variance sigma_s^2 >> sigma_w^2. ### R-P1 — Carleton stabilization points (FanGraphs Library)

**Citation.** Carleton, R. A. ("Pizza Cutter"), "Baseball Therapy: It's a Small Sample Size After All," Baseball Prospectus, July 16, 2012 (update of his 2007-08 StatSpeak split-half research), as documented on the FanGraphs Library "Sample Size" page (https://library.fangraphs.com/principles/sample-size/); supplemented by Dolinar, S. & Pemstein, J., "A New Way to Look at Sample Size: Math Supplement," FanGraphs, 2015 (https://blogs.fangraphs.com/a-new-way-to-look-at-sample-size-math-supplement/).

**Access used.** Full web page: FanGraphs Library "Sample Size" page (WebFetch, verified URL). Primary source: Carleton's Baseball Prospectus article "It's a Small Sample Size After All" (July 16, 2012) fetched in full — methodology quotes and hitter table verified there. Dolinar/Pemstein "A New Way to Look at Sample Size: Math Supplement" (FanGraphs) fetched for the alpha/regression formula. Pitcher stabilization numbers verified only from the FanGraphs Library page (the separate BP pitcher article was not fetched).

**Objective.** Determine, for each common batting and pitching rate statistic, how large a sample is needed before the observed rate is a reliable indicator of a player's underlying talent rather than noise. The practical goal, per the FanGraphs page, is "to prevent you from reacting to data that is still highly susceptible to random chance." The follow-up Dolinar/Pemstein work generalizes this from single thresholds to a continuous reliability curve usable for regression to the mean.

**Method.** Carleton's original research split each player's season into halves (evens-and-odds PA) and correlated the halves at increasing sample sizes. The 2012 update replaces single split-half with Kuder-Richardson 21 (a binary-outcome special case of Cronbach's alpha), which he describes as mathematically equivalent to averaging the correlations over every possible way of splitting the sample into two halves. For each stat he found the sample size where reliability passes 0.70, chosen because "At .70, the rate of signal to noise crosses the halfway point (.707 * .707) = 50%" (i.e., r=0.70 corresponds to R-squared of 0.49). Each stat is measured in its own natural denominator (PA, AB, BF, balls in play, or fly balls). Where 0.70 was not reached within the data set, the threshold was extrapolated with the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula. Dolinar/Pemstein compute Cronbach's alpha directly (alpha = 1 - sum of within-PA variance / between-player variance) and give the operational formula: Regressed Stat = alpha x Observed + (1 - alpha) x Sample Mean, i.e., the regression-to-the-mean weight is exactly (1 - alpha), turning reliability into a continuous shrinkage rule rather than a binary threshold.

**Results.** Hitter stabilization points (reliability 0.70): K% 60 PA; BB% 120 PA; HBP rate 240 PA; 1B rate 290 PA; HR rate 170 PA; XBH rate 1610 PA (Spearman-Brown estimate); OBP 460 PA; AVG 910 AB; SLG 320 AB; ISO 160 AB; GB% and FB% 80 BIP each; LD% 600 BIP; HR/FB 50 FB; BABIP 820 BIP. Pitcher stabilization points (per the FanGraphs page): K% 70 BF; BB% 170 BF; HBP 640 BF; 1B rate 670 BF; HR rate 1320 BF; XBH rate 1450 BF; AVG 630 BF; OBP 540 BF; SLG 550 AB; ISO 630 AB; GB%/FB% 70 BIP; LD% 650 BIP; HR/FB 400 FB; BABIP 2000 BIP. The ordering is the key structural finding: K and BB rates and GB/FB mix become reliable within weeks; HR rate is intermediate; anything routed through balls in play (BABIP, LD%, pitcher HR rate, XBH) needs one to multiple seasons. Pitcher contact-outcome stats are far less reliable than the same stats for hitters (pitcher BABIP 2000 BIP vs hitter 820). Dolinar/Pemstein additionally show reliability is driven by the ratio of between-player variance to within-PA variance, so low-spread stats (like BABIP) are inherently slow to stabilize.

**Conclusions.** Authors' conclusions: (1) "A statistic doesn't stabilize, it becomes more stable" — the thresholds are not magic lines where a stat "becomes real"; reliability grows continuously and the published numbers mark where signal roughly equals noise. (2) The correct use is regression to the mean with weight (1 - alpha), not accept/reject of observed rates. (3) Stabilization reflects talent during the sampled window only — Carleton stresses true talent "can change and does so more rapidly than we'd like to think." (4) Denominators matter: each threshold is in its own currency (PA vs BIP vs FB) and cannot be casually converted. Transferability note: this IS MLB data, so it transfers directly in that sense, but the estimates are from ~2012 player-level data; the run environment, strikeout rates, and ball behavior have shifted since, and the thresholds are player-level constants — team-level aggregates (E9) mix player talent and roster spread, so the project's team-rate ballast constants must be re-estimated on its own data rather than copied. Reading-note correction: the published thresholds are at reliability 0.70 (which Carleton equates to ~50% signal via r-squared), not at r=0.5; under an n/(n+k) reliability curve the true 50/50 shrinkage point sits at roughly 0.43x the published thresholds.

### R-P2 — Tango RE24 run-expectancy tables

**Citation.** Tango, T. "Run Expectancy Matrix, 1950-2015" (tangotiger.net/re24.html, Retrosheet data) and Tango, T. "SABR 201: Linear Weights by the 24 base/out states, 1999-2002" (tangotiger.net/RE9902event.html); supplemented by FanGraphs Library, "RE24" (library.fangraphs.com/misc/re24/).

**Access used.** Primary: WebFetch of http://www.tangotiger.net/re24.html (full page, two fetches covering all four era matrices) and https://tangotiger.net/RE9902event.html (per-event values, found via WebSearch — the re24 page itself contains no outbound links). Secondary: FanGraphs Library RE24 page (library.fangraphs.com/misc/re24/) for the per-play RE24 formula. No RE288 page was linked or located from the primary page.

**Objective.** Provide the empirical base-out run expectancy (RE24) matrix — the expected number of runs scored from each of the 24 base/out states to the end of the inning — computed from MLB play-by-play across multiple eras, so that the run environment's state dependence can be measured. The companion linear-weights page derives per-event run values (HR, 3B, 2B, 1B, BB, HBP, out, K, etc.) as run-expectancy deltas, both averaged overall and broken out by each of the 24 base/out states.

**Method.** RE matrices are built from Retrosheet play-by-play, 1950-2015, restricted to completed innings through the 8th inning (to avoid truncation and walk-off bias). For each of the 24 base/out states, the value is the average number of runs that scored from that state to the end of the inning. Four era-specific matrices are presented (1950-1968, 1969-1992, 1993-2009, 2010-2015) to capture changes in run environment. Per-event run values (the 1999-2002 page) are computed as RE(end state) − RE(start state) + runs scored on the play, i.e., "take the run expectancy, by base/out/event, and subtract the run expectancy by base/out"; averaging these deltas over all instances of an event yields its linear-weights run value, and conditioning on the starting base/out state yields a 24-state-specific value per event. Tango notes that because the subtracted figures come from different empirical samples, state-level values can be noisy, and he recommends "a full Markov approach, but tailored to the run environment in question" as the cleaner alternative.

**Results.** 2010-2015 RE24 matrix (0/1/2 outs): empty .481/.254/.098; 1B .859/.509/.224; 2B 1.100/.664/.319; 1B+2B 1.437/.884/.429; 3B 1.350/.950/.353; 1B+3B 1.784/1.130/.478; 2B+3B 1.964/1.376/.580; loaded 2.292/1.541/.752. Era dependence is material: the 1993-2009 high-offense era runs higher (empty/0 outs .547, loaded/0 outs 2.390) while 1969-1992 (empty/0 .477) and 1950-1968 (empty/0 .476) resemble 2010-2015. Average per-event run values from 1999-2002 RE deltas: HR 1.409, 3B 1.063, 2B 0.764, 1B 0.474, ROE 0.546, HBP 0.385, unintentional BB 0.33, IBB 0.102, out −0.299, strikeout −0.31. State-conditional values vary several-fold: HR ranges from ~1.0 (empty, 0 outs; e.g., 1.591 − 0.555 ≈ 1.04) to 3.31 (loaded, 2 outs); singles from 0.13 (empty, 2 outs) to 1.53 (loaded, 2 outs); walks from 0.13 to 1.06 over the same extremes. The RE24-per-play formula (per FanGraphs) is RE(end) − RE(start) + runs scored on the play.

**Conclusions.** Run values of batting events are strongly conditional on the base-out state and on era/run environment, so a single context-free value per outcome is an approximation; empirical state-level deltas carry sampling noise, and Tango's own recommendation for generating expected runs is a full Markov model tailored to the current run environment. Linear weights derived this way are the standard foundation for wOBA-style valuation (though neither fetched page spells out the wOBA rescaling). Transferability is not an issue — this is MLB Retrosheet data — but the published matrices end at 2015 and the event values are from the 1999-2002 high-offense era, so the project should recompute era-appropriate, point-in-time matrices from its own play-by-play rather than hardcoding these numbers.

**Proposed experiments:**
- **B8a-check: RE-implied expected-RBI sanity table** (→ B8a; metric: Absolute deviation between model r̄(outcome, slot) and RE-implied expected RBI per (outcome, slot) cell; flag cells off by more than sampling error.)

No model change; a validation gate.

### R3-A — Soto-Valero 2016 (IJCSS)

**Citation.** Soto Valero, C. (2016). "Predicting Win-Loss outcomes in MLB regular season games – A comparative study using data mining methods." International Journal of Computer Science in Sport, 15(2), 91–112. DOI: 10.1515/ijcss-2016-0007. Candidate URL verified correct (abstract page); full text at https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.1515/ijcss-2016-0007.pdf and https://www.sciendo.com/article/10.1515/ijcss-2016-0007.

**Access used.** Candidate URL (reference-global.com article page) served abstract-only via WebFetch. DOI 10.1515/ijcss-2016-0007 redirects to sciendo.com. Full text obtained from the publisher mirror's direct PDF (https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.1515/ijcss-2016-0007.pdf), downloaded via WebFetch and text-extracted locally with pypdf (all 22 pages read, including the full accuracy table, feature ranking table, and appendices). All numbers quoted come from that primary full text; no secondary sources needed.

**Objective.** Assess the predictive capability of four data mining method families (lazy learners/kNN, artificial neural networks, SVMs, decision trees) for predicting MLB regular-season game outcomes from the home team's perspective, using sabermetric statistics. Compares two schemes: classification (Win/Loss label) vs regression (run differential, sign taken as the pick). Also characterizes the intrinsic geometric complexity of the problem and benchmarks the best model against the Moneyline betting market.

**Method.** Data: Retrosheet game logs plus the Lahman database, seasons 2005–2014 (ten years), 1620 game records per team, organized into 30 per-team datasets (one model per team). A Java parser stripped batter/coach/umpire identifiers and built day-by-day CUMULATIVE season statistics; each game instance uses only statistics accumulated PRIOR to that game (explicit "data contamination" discussion — e.g., game length in outs is a strong but contaminated predictor and was excluded). Features are home-minus-visitor differences of accumulative stats (WPDiff, PE diff, OPS diff, Runs Created diff, etc.). Feature selection in WEKA used five attribute evaluators (Significance, Chi-squared, Pearson correlation, Gain ratio, Relief) with majority-vote rank averaging, yielding a reduced set of 60 input variables. Algorithms (WEKA implementations): 1-NN (lazy), MLP with one hidden layer (ANN), REPTree (decision/regression tree), and SMO (SVM), each run in both classification and regression mode. Evaluation: stratified 10-fold cross-validation per team, accuracy as the metric; method differences tested with Aligned Friedman + Hochberg post-hoc, schemes compared with Wilcoxon signed-rank. Problem complexity was quantified with two geometry-based measures, maximum Fisher's discriminant ratio (F1) and maximum feature efficiency (F3), via KEEL.

**Results.** Mean 10-fold CV accuracies across the 30 teams: 1-NN 55.98% (classification) / 55.97% (regression); MLP 57.89% / 56.17%; REPTree 57.86% / 57.90%; SMO (SVM) 58.92% / 57.66% (SMO STDs 1.64 and 1.82). SMO was the best method (Aligned Friedman p=3.06e-5 classification, 2.18e-5 regression; Hochberg rejects all comparisons in SMO's favor except REPTree under regression). Classification significantly beat regression for SMO (Wilcoxon p=7.2e-5). Best per-team values: PIT 62.27% and COL 61.50% (SMO classification); a WAS 1-NN peak of 61.26/62.08% is called an outlier by the author. ### R3-P1 — FanGraphs Stuff+/Location+/Pitching+ primer

**Citation.** Eno Sarris (created with Max Bay; currently maintained by Sarris and Owen McGrattan, engineering by Matt Dennewitz), "Stuff+, Location+, and Pitching+ Primer," FanGraphs Sabermetrics Library, https://library.fangraphs.com/pitching/stuff-location-and-pitching-primer/ (corrected from candidate URL blogs.fangraphs.com/stuff-location-and-pitching-primer/, which does not resolve to the primer). Companion introduction: "PitchingBot and Stuff+ Pitch Modeling Is Now on FanGraphs!", blogs.fangraphs.com.

**Access used.** WebSearch to locate the correct URL (candidate URL was wrong); WebFetch of the full primer at https://library.fangraphs.com/pitching/stuff-location-and-pitching-primer/; WebFetch of the FanGraphs introduction article https://blogs.fangraphs.com/pitchingbot-and-stuff-pitch-modeling-are-now-on-fangraphs/. A WebSearch snippet (secondary, unverified against a primary update post) indicates the Stuff+ model was later retrained as a classification model similar to PitchingBot.

**Objective.** Explain a family of three process-based pitching metrics that judge the quality of a pitcher's inputs rather than his results, to strip the noise (defense, sequencing, luck) out of small-sample pitcher evaluation. Stuff+ grades the physical characteristics of each pitch, Location+ grades where pitches are thrown given count and pitch type, and Pitching+ combines both into an overall process grade. The explicit motivation is that process metrics stabilize far faster than results-based metrics like ERA/FIP, making them usable in tiny samples.

**Method.** All three are decision-tree-based models scored per pitch and trained against run values per pitch (a secondary source indicates a later retrain as a classification model over pitch outcomes, PitchingBot-style, which handles balls in play more directly). Stuff+ uses only physical pitch characteristics: release point, velocity, vertical and horizontal movement, spin rate, and axis differential (seam-shifted wake); it deliberately excludes location, so deceptive release/movement combinations can score well without elite velocity. Location+ ignores physical characteristics entirely and uses only pitch location, adjusted for count and pitch type. Pitching+ is a third unified model (not a weighted average of the other two) using physical characteristics, location, count, and batter handedness (capturing platoon effects on movement and location). Per-pitch scores are aggregated to pitcher level on a wRC+-style scale where 100 = league average for each pitch type and 10 points = one standard deviation at the pitch level. Aggregate standard deviations differ by role: Stuff+ SD is 12.16 for SP and 17.02 for RP; Location+ 3.34/5.87; Pitching+ 4.94/6.61.

**Results.** The primer's central quantitative claims are about stabilization speed, not correlation coefficients (no r/r-squared values are published). Stuff+ "becomes reliable 80 pitches into the season" and is described as extremely powerful relative to any other single stat in the tiniest of samples; Location+ needs roughly 400 pitches to reach similar stability (reliability assessed via Cronbach's alpha, targeting ~0.9). In-season Pitching+ out-predicts preseason projection systems after ~250 pitches for relievers and ~400 pitches (4-5 starts) for starters; preseason Pitching+ is claimed to have lower RMSE against on-field ERA than most projection systems, and Pitching+ reaches reliability and predicts rest-of-season results faster than K-BB%. Year-to-year, Stuff+ carries most of the stickiness; Location+ is less sticky. Role effects are quantified: relievers converting to starting should expect roughly a 5.5-point Stuff+ drop. The introduction article adds that BABIP correlates with Stuff+, i.e., pitchers exert some control over balls in play.

**Conclusions.** Authors conclude that process-based pitch grades separate skill from noise much faster than results metrics: Stuff+ is the safest early-sample and year-to-year signal, location matters more than stuff on any single pitch but is less stable as a skill, and the longer a pitcher's MLB track record, the more actual results should be weighted against Pitching+. Transferability is direct: this is MLB, built on the same Statcast physical quantities (velocity, movement, spin, location) already in our statcast_pitches table — it is essentially a recipe for the in-house SP-quality feature family the project has queued, targeting exactly our weakness: results-based SP features (SP_ERA_L10, SP_WOBA_AGAINST_L10) are noisy in March-June, the window where our picks trail the market and where picks ride on the SP block + Elo.
